Joint Handover and Partial Task Offloading in LEO
Satellite Edge Networks via Heterogeneous

Abstract—Integrating low Earth orbit (LEO) satellites with
edge computing extends computation services to areas with
limited terrestrial coverage and to terminals with constrained
local resources. However, satellite motion continually changes
link quality, satellite visibility, and remaining service time, while
multiple users compete for limited onboard communication and
computing resources. For divisible tasks, a user must select a
serving satellite and determine a continuous offloading ratio
matched to that satellite’s link, resource, and contention con-
ditions. We therefore formulate joint serving-satellite selection
and partial task offloading as a sequential decision problem with
a parameterized hybrid action space. To solve this problem, we
propose HAN–PDQN, which combines a heterogeneous graph
attention network (HAN) with a parameterized deep Q-network
(PDQN). A heterogeneous graph distinguishes user and satel-
lite nodes as well as serving and visibility relations. Relation-
specific meta-path aggregation and hierarchical attention capture
established resource sharing and potential contention among
users with overlapping satellite alternatives. Conditioned on this
relational representation, local task information, and candidate-
satellite features, PDQN generates a continuous offloading ratio
for each feasible satellite action and estimates the long-term value
of the resulting satellite–ratio pair. The same contention context
therefore affects both how much workload is assigned to each
candidate and whether that candidate is selected. HAN–PDQN
consequently supports coordinated serving-satellite selection and
partial task offloading in dynamic multi-user LEO satellite edge
networks.Simulations across five training seeds and network sizes
of 20–40 users show that, compared with PDQN, HAN–PDQN
reduces average successful-task delay, energy per successful task,
and the deadline violation rate by 2.16%, 5.72%, and 21.26%,
respectively, while increasing the task success rate by 2.12%.
Index Terms—Heterogeneous graph attention, joint handover
and offloading, LEO satellite edge computing, parameterized
deep reinforcement learning, partial task offloading.
I. INTRODUCTION
Ubiquitous connectivity and computing are important objec-
tives of sixth-generation (6G) networks. However, terrestrial
infrastructure cannot consistently provide edge services in
remote regions, maritime environments, and other areas with
limited network coverage. Satellite edge computing can com-
plement terrestrial systems by extending communication and
computation services to these areas [1]. In particular, low Earth
orbit (LEO) satellites offer shorter propagation distances than
satellites in higher orbits and can host onboard edge servers to
process tasks from ground terminals. These capabilities make
LEO satellite edge computing a promising option for users
with limited local computing resources.
Providing sustained computing services in LEO networks
requires coordinated decisions on satellite association and
workload allocation. Satellite motion continuously changes
the visible satellite set, link quality, and remaining service
time, making handovers necessary to maintain service [2].
Meanwhile, multiple users share the limited uplink bandwidth
and computing resources of each satellite. For divisible tasks,
a user must decide both which satellite to select and what frac-
tion of its workload to offload, while processing the remaining
fraction locally in parallel. A suitable offloading ratio depends
on the selected satellite’s link conditions, computing capacity,
queued workload, and remaining visibility. Conversely, users’
offloading ratios change resource occupancy and the execu-
tion conditions experienced by other users. Under stochastic
task arrivals, unfinished workloads carry these effects across
time slots, while handovers may interrupt service and require
workload migration. Serving-satellite selection and partial task
offloading must therefore be considered jointly: the former
determines remote execution conditions, whereas the latter
determines the workload assigned to those conditions.
Existing studies provide two complementary foundations
for addressing this coupled decision problem. Graph-based
resource management and satellite handover methods use
relational representations to organize network interactions and
support access decisions [3], [4]. Such representations are use-
ful for identifying service opportunities and resource-sharing
relationships, but satellite selection alone does not specify the
continuous task fraction to assign to the selected server. Mean-
while, satellite edge computing studies have investigated joint
access selection, offloading, and resource allocation [5], [6],
including multi-agent and hierarchical reinforcement learning
approaches to dynamic multi-user control [7]–[9]. Parameter-
ized reinforcement learning further supports discrete decisions
with associated continuous controls [10]. For example, an
existing satellite formulation selects among local, satellite, and
cloud execution modes while allocating computing resources
and transmission power [11]. These advances motivate a more
specific design question for partial offloading: how should
the relationships among competing users inform both the
workload proposed for each candidate satellite and the value
of selecting that satellite? Addressing this question requires in-
tegrating relational contention modeling with satellite-specific
discrete–continuous control.
Achieving this integration gives rise to two technical chal-
lenges. The first is how to represent dynamic multi-user
contention. Users currently connected to the same satellite ex-
perience established contention for shared communication and
computing resources. Users connected to different satellites
2
may also face potential contention when their visible satellite
sets overlap and subsequent decisions direct their workloads
to the same server. An aggregate load indicator does not
explicitly preserve these different relationships or identify the
task demands contributing to them. The state representation
must therefore retain node heterogeneity, relation semantics,
and unequal neighbor importance. Accurately representing
this contention, however, is only part of the problem. The
controller must also jointly optimize a discrete satellite choice
and its continuous offloading ratio. The relevant action is a
pair(s, ρ s), wheresdenotes the serving-satellite choice and
ρs is its associated task fraction. Because execution conditions
differ across candidates, the ratio must be satellite-specific. A
conventional discrete-action deep Q-network (DQN) would re-
quire discretizing the ratio, whereas independently determining
the satellite and workload fraction can overlook their mutual
dependence. Moreover, handover interruptions and residual
workloads affect subsequent service conditions, making the
problem sequential rather than a collection of independent
single-slot optimizations.
To address these challenges, we propose HAN–PDQN,
which combines a heterogeneous graph attention network
(HAN) with a parameterized deep Q-network (PDQN) for joint
serving-satellite selection and partial task offloading. HAN
provides a contention-aware relational representation of the
user’s service environment. Conditioned on this representa-
tion, PDQN generates an offloading ratio for each satellite
action and estimates the long-term value of the corresponding
satellite–ratio pair. The framework thus connects information
about competing users and satellite resources to both workload
allocation and satellite selection within a unified decision
process.
To capture the heterogeneous contention relationships iden-
tified above, we construct a graph with user and satellite
nodes and organize their interactions through five types of
meta-paths. On the user side, connectivity-based and visibility-
based U–S–U meta-paths characterize established and poten-
tial contention, respectively, while a U–U path incorporates
neighboring-user information. On the satellite side, S–U–S
and S–S–S meta-paths describe overlapping service opportuni-
ties and inter-satellite topological dependencies. These meta-
paths provide complementary views of user demand, service
alternatives, and satellite connectivity. Following hierarchical
attention-based aggregation [12], node-level attention weights
contributing neighbors within each meta-path, while semantic-
level attention combines the applicable meta-path representa-
tions for each node type. The resulting user representation
organizes task and resource information through these distinct
relationships, with serving and visibility paths explicitly dis-
tinguishing established from potential contention.
Given this contention-aware relational representation,
PDQN then addresses the action-coupling challenge by com-
bining the HAN output with local task information and
candidate-satellite features. Following the parameterized ac-
tion formulation [10], action-specific parameter branches gen-
erate continuous offloading ratios for the available satellite
actions, including retaining the current association. The Q-
network evaluates the same state together with a satellite
action and its corresponding ratio to estimate the cumulative
return of the complete decision. Thus, the same relational
contention information influences both how much workload a
candidate should receive and whether that candidate should be
selected. A feasibility mask excludes actions disallowed by the
current connection and handover conditions, and the selected
satellite action and its associated ratio are executed together.
By evaluating cumulative returns, the controller accounts for
immediate handover costs and subsequent task outcomes while
balancing task completion, processing delay, terminal energy
consumption, service continuity, and satellite resource utiliza-
tion.
The main contributions of this work are summarized as
follows:
•We formulate joint serving-satellite selection and partial
task offloading as a sequential decision problem with a
parameterized hybrid action space. Each discrete satellite
action is associated with a continuous workload fraction.
The formulation accounts for stochastic task arrivals,
time-varying visibility, shared communication and com-
puting resources, parallel local and remote execution, and
handover interruptions with unfinished workloads.
•We develop a heterogeneous relational representation of
the multi-user satellite service environment. User-side
connectivity, visibility, and neighbor meta-paths are com-
plemented by satellite-side visibility and inter-satellite-
link meta-paths. Hierarchical attention integrates these
relationships into contention-aware representations, ex-
plicitly distinguishing established resource sharing from
potential competition through common satellite alterna-
tives.
•We develop HAN–PDQN, a joint controller in which the
same relational representation conditions both satellite-
specific offloading-ratio generation and satellite–ratio
value estimation. This design connects multi-user con-
tention modeling to the joint selection of the destination
and amount of offloaded work according to their long-
term consequences.

IV. PROPOSEDHAN–PDQN FRAMEWORK
A. Framework Overview
To address (22), we develop HAN–PDQN, which connects
a representation of multi-user contention to parameterized
satellite and offloading decisions. As illustrated in Fig. 2, the
controller constructs a heterogeneous graph from user demand,
satellite resources, and their serving and visibility relations.
HAN aggregates these measurements into a user representation
that distinguishes established resource sharing from potential
competition through overlapping satellite alternatives.
The representation is combined with the user’s local task
and candidate-satellite observations. Conditioned on this input,
PDQN generates an offloading fraction for each satellite action
and evaluates the resulting satellite–fraction pairs. All users
share the decision-network parameters, while their observa-
tions and feasible candidates produce different actions. The
joint requests are then executed under the service rules in
Section II, yielding task outcomes and the next network
snapshot. Sections IV-B and IV-C develop the representation
and decision mechanisms, respectively; Section IV-D describes
training and online execution.
B. Heterogeneous Graph-Based Contention Representation
At slott, we constructG t = (V,E t,X t,E t), whereV
contains user and satellite nodes,E t contains typed edges,
andX t andE t collect node and edge features. User features
describe location, connection status, task demand, urgency, and
backlog. Satellite features describe location, MEC utilization,
queue occupancy, associated users, and available computing
resources. Visibility edges carry distance, elevation, SNR,
reference rate, and remaining visibility; serving edges iden-
tify existing associations and their service duration. Nearby-
user and ISL edges provide geographical and constellation-
neighborhood information. User–satellite relations are repre-
sented in both directions.

V. PERFORMANCEEVALUATION
A. Experimental Setup and Evaluation Protocol
We evaluate HAN–PDQN in a multi-user LEO satellite
edge computing network comprising 66 satellites distributed
across six orbital planes. The orbital altitude and inclination
are 550 km and53 ◦, respectively. Ground users are distributed
around(39.9 ◦N,116.4 ◦E), and the population varies from
20 to 40 users. User locations and orbital starting times are
resampled at episode initialization. Each episode contains 512
slots, with a slot duration of 1 s. Tables II and III summarize
the system and training parameters, respectively.
Each user generates a task independently with probabil-
ity 0.35 per slot. Tasks are classified as light, medium,
and heavy with probabilities 0.3, 0.5, and 0.2. For light
tasks, the input size, computation demand, and deadline
are uniformly sampled from[1,3]Mbit,[0.3,0.8]Gcycle,
and[1.5,3]s, respectively. The corresponding ranges are
[5,10]Mbit,[1.2,2.5]Gcycle, and[2.5,5.5]s for medium
tasks, and[12,25]Mbit,[3,7]Gcycle, and[4,8]s for
heavy tasks. Each MEC provides a total processing rate of
10 Gcycle/s through two 5-GHz cores. The utility weights
are(ω D, ωE, ωI, ωF, ωL) = (0.60,0.40,0.30,0.20,0.05), with
Eref = 1J.
We compare HAN–PDQN with six benchmarks:
•HAN+MAPPO: A graph-assisted multi-agent proximal
policy optimization controller for joint satellite selection
and offloading.
•MAPPO: A multi-agent proximal policy optimization
controller using raw user, task, and candidate-satellite
observations.
•MADDPG: A multi-agent deep deterministic policy gra-
dient controller using raw observations to generate satel-
lite and offloading decisions.
•PDQN: A parameterized Q-learning controller using raw
observations without HAN representations.
•Min-Distance: A distance-based policy that requests the
nearest admissible satellite under the handover conditions
and requests full offloading. It retains the current associ-
ation when no shorter admissible alternative is available.
•Full-Local: A policy that processes all tasks locally and
issues no handover requests.
All methods operate under the same task, communication,
MEC service, and admission models.
For each user population, the learning methods are trained
with seeds 42–46 and an interaction budget of1.5×10 5
environment steps per run. Table III reports the principal
training settings. HAN–PDQN and PDQN use replay batch
sizes of 512 and 128, respectively. Periodic model evaluation
is performed every 25,000 environment steps for the PDQN
and MAPPO variants, and every ten episodes for MADDPG.
Checkpoints are selected according to their mean evaluation
return.
Each selected policy is evaluated over five episodes per
training seed, using matched scenario seeds across methods.
Performance metrics are first averaged over these episodes and
then summarized across the five training seeds. Shaded regions
and error bars represent 95% bootstrap confidence intervals at
the training-seed level.

delay is averaged over successful tasks, whereas the deadline
violation rate is normalized by the number of generated

C. Task Performance under User Scaling
Fig. 5 evaluates task service as the user population increases
from 20 to 40. The per-user arrival process remains unchanged,
so a larger population increases the aggregate demand for the
shared uplink and MEC resources. HAN–PDQN achieves the
lowest mean successful-task delay, deadline violation rate, and
energy per successful task at every evaluated population.
In Fig. 5(a), the mean successful-task delay of HAN–PDQN
increases from 1554.9 ms at 20 users to 1906.7 ms at 40 users.
The corresponding PDQN values are 1608.2 and 1939.9 ms.
At 30 users, HAN–PDQN achieves 1779.7 ms, compared with
1820.7 ms for PDQN and 2053.8 ms for HAN+MAPPO. The
increase with population is consistent with greater contention
for upload bandwidth and MEC service. Throughout this
range, the proposed controller maintains a lower delay despite
the growing demand.
The reliability difference is more pronounced in Fig. 5(b).
At 30 users, the deadline violation rate is 6.89% for HAN–
PDQN and 9.47% for PDQN, a reduction of 2.58 percent-
age points. HAN+MAPPO and MAPPO yield 18.38% and
18.83%, respectively, under the same population. At 40 users,
HAN–PDQN maintains a violation rate of 10.99%, compared
with 13.08% for PDQN, 24.24% for HAN+MAPPO, and
37.47% for Min-Distance. These results show that the delay
advantage is accompanied by improved deadline compliance.
Fig. 5(c) further shows that HAN–PDQN uses less terminal
energy per successful task across all evaluated populations. Its
value increases from 0.948 J at 20 users to 1.491 J at 40 users,
while PDQN increases from 1.040 to 1.537 J. At 30 users,
HAN–PDQN requires 1.242 J per successful task, compared
with 1.318 J for PDQN and 2.376 J for Min-Distance. The
combination of lower delay, fewer deadline violations, and
lower energy per successful task indicates an improvement
across the three task-service dimensions.

D. Service Continuity and Operating Costs
Fig. 6 complements the task-level results by examining
service interruption, aggregate terminal energy, and handover
activity. These metrics reveal how different association and
offloading policies balance connection availability against the
conditions under which tasks are executed.
In Fig. 6(a), HAN–PDQN has lower mean blocked user-time
than PDQN at every population. At 30 users, the values are
284.08 and 298.12 user-s per episode, respectively. The cumu-
lative quantity increases with population, partly because more
users contribute to the total observation time. Min-Distance
yields a slightly lower value of 276.32 user-s at 30 users, but its
deadline violation rate in Fig. 5(b) is much higher. Connection
availability therefore needs to be considered together with the
processing conditions and workload assigned to the selected
satellite.
Fig. 6(b) shows that HAN–PDQN also maintains the lowest
mean total terminal energy over the evaluated populations.
At 30 users, it consumes 6.18 kJ per episode, compared
with 6.37 kJ for PDQN, 8.41 kJ for Min-Distance, and
11.26 kJ for Full-Local. Total energy measures the system’s
accumulated terminal expenditure, whereas Fig. 5(c) measures
the expenditure per successful task. The improvement in both
quantities shows that the energy-efficiency gain accompanies
a reduction in aggregate terminal consumption.
The handover results in Fig. 6(c) show a different ordering
among the methods. At 30 users, HAN–PDQN performs
0.606 handovers per user-minute, compared with 0.659 for
PDQN, 0.192 for HAN+MAPPO, and 0.198 for Min-Distance.
Thus, HAN–PDQN obtains its task-performance advantage
with fewer handovers than PDQN, while remaining more
active in satellite reassociation than HAN+MAPPO and Min-
Distance. The lower switching activity of the latter policies
is accompanied by higher task delay, deadline violations, and
energy per successful task. This comparison reflects the role
of the long-term objective in balancing immediate switching
costs against subsequent task-service conditions.
Using the same across-population averaging convention as
in Section V-C, HAN–PDQN reduces mean blocked user-
time, total terminal energy, and handover frequency relative
to PDQN by 7.85%, 3.19%, and 14.82%, respectively. These
operating-cost reductions accompany the task-level improve-
ments, linking the quality of completed service to the associ-
ation and workload decisions that sustain it.
VI. CONCLUSION
In this paper, we investigated joint handover and partial
task offloading in multi-user LEO satellite edge networks.
We formulated satellite selection and continuous workload
allocation as a sequential decision problem and developed
HAN–PDQN to account for dynamic connectivity and shared
resources. HAN distinguishes established contention through
serving relations from potential contention through common
satellite alternatives. The resulting representation conditions
both the offloading fraction generated for each candidate and
the long-term value of the corresponding satellite–fraction pair.
12
This design connects the destination and amount of offloaded
work through the same multi-user contention context.
Simulations over five training seeds and user populations
of 20–40 showed that, compared with PDQN, HAN–PDQN
reduced average successful-task delay, deadline violation rate,
and energy per successful task by 2.16%, 21.26%, and 5.72%,
respectively. These task-level improvements were accompa-
nied by reductions in blocked user-time, total terminal energy,
and handover frequency. The results demonstrate the effective-
ness of the proposed framework in balancing task reliability,
processing delay, and terminal energy across the evaluated
user populations. Future work will extend the framework
to jointly allocate communication and computing resources
