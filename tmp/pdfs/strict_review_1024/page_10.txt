We next examine the sensitivity of HAN–PDQN to its
learning rate and replay batch size in Fig. 4. For the learning-
rate experiment, the batch size is fixed at 512 and the rate
is selected from{10 −4,3×10 −4,10 −3,3×10 −3}. For the
batch-size experiment, the learning rate is fixed at10 −3 and
the batch size is selected from{128,256,512,1024}. Each
setting uses five training seeds.
In Fig. 4(a), learning rates between10 −4 and10 −3 attain
similar late-stage returns. Increasing the rate to3×10 −3
produces a substantially lower mean return and a wider con-
fidence interval. This behavior indicates sensitivity to aggres-
sive updates when the value estimates and continuous action
parameters evolve together.
Fig. 4(b) shows that batch sizes 128, 512, and 1024 reach
comparable return levels, whereas batch size 256 yields a
lower mean and greater variation across seeds. The dependence
on batch size is therefore nonmonotonic over the examined
settings. The default combination of learning rate10 −3 and
batch size 512 attains a high return with a consistent five-seed
training profile and is used for the subsequent HAN–PDQN
comparisons.
C. Task Performance under User Scaling
Fig. 5 evaluates task service as the user population increases
from 20 to 40. The per-user arrival process remains unchanged,
so a larger population increases the aggregate demand for the
shared uplink and MEC resources. HAN–PDQN achieves the
lowest mean successful-task delay, deadline violation rate, and
energy per successful task at every evaluated population.
In Fig. 5(a), the mean successful-task delay of HAN–PDQN
increases from 1554.9 ms at 20 users to 1906.7 ms at 40 users.
The corresponding PDQN values are 1608.2 and 1939.9 ms.
At 30 users, HAN–PDQN achieves 1779.7 ms, compared with
1820.7 ms for PDQN and 2053.8 ms for HAN+MAPPO. The
increase with population is consistent with greater contention
for upload bandwidth and MEC service. Throughout this
range, the proposed controller maintains a lower delay despite
the growing demand.
The reliability difference is more pronounced in Fig. 5(b).
At 30 users, the deadline violation rate is 6.89% for HAN–
PDQN and 9.47% for PDQN, a reduction of 2.58 percent-
age points. HAN+MAPPO and MAPPO yield 18.38% and
18.83%, respectively, under the same population. At 40 users,
HAN–PDQN maintains a violation rate of 10.99%, compared
with 13.08% for PDQN, 24.24% for HAN+MAPPO, and
37.47% for Min-Distance. These results show that the delay
advantage is accompanied by improved deadline compliance.
Fig. 5(c) further shows that HAN–PDQN uses less terminal
energy per successful task across all evaluated populations. Its
value increases from 0.948 J at 20 users to 1.491 J at 40 users,
while PDQN increases from 1.040 to 1.537 J. At 30 users,
HAN–PDQN requires 1.242 J per successful task, compared
with 1.318 J for PDQN and 2.376 J for Min-Distance. The
combination of lower delay, fewer deadline violations, and
lower energy per successful task indicates an improvement
across the three task-service dimensions.
